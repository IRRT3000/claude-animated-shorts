## Facts (checked 2026-11-06; popular/press sources, wording kept safe where sources disagree)
- Joaquín de la Cantolla y Rico, Mexico City, born 1829 (some say 1837), died 1914. Studied at the Colegio Militar, worked on the
  first telegraph lines (telegraphist). (El Universal 2016 "El telegrafista que quiso surcar los cielos"; HMDB marker 129131)
- From 1862-63 he built his own balloons: Moctezuma I, Moctezuma II, Vulcano. 1863 ascent from the Plaza de Toros del Paseo Nuevo,
  under Maximilian. (El Universal "Cantolla, el aeronauta"; panchovillamx.com; komoni.chemisax.com)
- He showed up dressed as a charro, on horseback, then dismounted and boarded. ("Flew WITH the horse" = legend → NOT used.)
  (afuegolento.com; panchovillamx.com; mexicanisimo.com.mx)
- A cold downdraft dropped his balloon through the skylight/roof of a house on Salto del Agua street; he "cayó parado"
  (landed on his feet) and the family, once over the fright, beat him. No date in sources → no year said.
  (komoni.chemisax.com; zonafranca.mx; heroesvillanosyartistas.blogspot.com)
- 1914: last ascent with aviator Alberto Braniff (INAH Mediateca photos 448435 / 448336 show both in the balloon). Wind carried them
  toward the Chalco valley, Zapatista territory; accounts say Zapatista troops fired at the balloon; they came down safely.
  He died days later (stroke; 20 Mar 1914 most cited). (elhistoriador.mx; nvinoticias.com; siempre.mx 2014)
- Paper hot-air lanterns released at Mexican fiestas are called "globos de Cantolla / Cantoya" after him.
  (Academia Mexicana de la Lengua, "Globos de cantolla")
- Physics: warm air is less dense than the cooler air around it, so the envelope is pushed up (buoyancy), same as a ball under water.

## Script
In Mexico City, a man fell out of the sky, straight through a family's roof... and the family beat him up.
His name was Joaquín de la Cantolla. By day, he tapped out telegrams. By night, he built balloons.
In 1863, a bullring full of people watched him ride in, dressed as a charro, climb into a balloon he had made himself... and go up.
He called it Moctezuma. And it actually flew.
So he built another one. And another. Higher over the city, longer over the rooftops,
until one day, a gust of cold air pushed him down, through a skylight, on Salto del Agua street.
Luckily, he landed on his feet.
Unluckily, he landed in somebody's dining room.
Here's how he kept getting up there. Hot air is lighter than the cold air around it.
Ever let go of a beach ball under water? It shoots straight up.
A hot-air balloon is a beach ball, and the sky is the pool.
He kept flying for fifty years.
In 1914, now an old man, he went up one last time, with the aviator Alberto Braniff.
The wind carried them over rebel country... and the soldiers below opened fire.
They came down alive. A few days later, back home, his heart gave out.
But he never really came down.
Every year, all over Mexico, thousands of little paper hot-air balloons float up into the night.
People still call them by his name.

           glide beside the striped balloon "Moctezuma" swaying, the charro hat flapping.

           then the bell tower itself sweeps past camera-near as the balloon drops (parallax, depth blur).
2.0-3.3 s  Downdraft: the balloon sinks fast, washing lines on the azoteas whip past, papel picado strings snap; camera follows down.
3.3-4.0 s  KICK: crash through the skylight (glass-shard fx cards, white flash at "roof").
4.0-5.5 s  Inside: Joaquín lands standing on the dinner table; the family freezes mid-bite, then the abuela's broom ("beat him up").
