# "The Summer Everyone Danced": original script v1 (2026-11-06)
Format: cartoon-3d-short (~65-70 s), one steady voice, English only, no captions. Our own characters and story.

## Facts (checked 2026-11-06 by web search; wikipedia/publicdomainreview fetches are blocked here, so each fact is cross-checked in 2+ reports)
- July 1518, Strasbourg (then Holy Roman Empire, now France): a woman known as Frau Troffea began dancing alone in the
  street and kept going for days (4-6 days in most accounts); her feet swelled and bled. (Wikipedia; National Geographic;
  History Skills; Public Domain Review)
- Within about a week some three dozen others had joined; by August the dancers numbered, by some counts, up to ~400.
  Death tolls are disputed (city records name none), so the script claims no deaths. (Wikipedia; PopSci; allthatsinteresting)
- Physicians blamed "hot blood" and prescribed more dancing. The council turned guild halls into dance floors, cleared the
  grain market and built a wooden stage by the horse market, hired musicians and, per some sources, paid strong men to
  hold dancers upright. (Daniel Specklin's chronicle via Public Domain Review; John Waller, "A Time to Dance, a Time to
  Die"; Wikipedia; Culture Trip)
- It backfired: more people joined. The council reversed course, tore down the stages, sent the musicians home and
  banned public dancing (and music). (Wikipedia; allthatsinteresting; History Skills)
- The dancers were taken by wagon to a shrine of Saint Vitus in the hills near Saverne; priests gave them red shoes and
  anointed them with holy oil and water. It faded by early September. (Waller via Public Domain Review; bygonesociety;
  National Geographic)
- Modern view (Waller): mass psychogenic illness in a city worn down by famine and disease, which believed Saint Vitus
  could curse sinners with dancing. Ergot poisoning is a rival theory, disputed. (National Geographic; ScienceDirect
  "In a spin", Waller 2008)
- Analogy: contagious yawning (seeing someone yawn makes you yawn) is a documented, everyday effect.

## Script
In fifteen eighteen, a whole city caught a disease that made people dance. The doctors' cure? More dancing.
Strasbourg, July. A woman named Frau Troffea stepped into the street and started dancing.
No music. No reason. And she didn't stop for days.
Within a week, more than thirty neighbors had joined her.
By August, by some counts, four hundred people were dancing day and night, on bleeding feet.
The doctors had a diagnosis: hot blood. And the cure for hot blood, they said, was to dance it out.
So the council cleared the guild halls, built a wooden stage in the horse market, hired musicians, and even paid strong men to hold the dancers up.
Luckily, the music worked. Unluckily, it worked on everyone.
So the council spun around. They tore down the stage, sent the band home, and banned dancing in public.
Historians think it was mass panic, in a hungry, sick city that feared a saint who cursed people with dancing.
It spread like a yawn. See someone yawn, you yawn. See your neighbor dance, your feet start moving.
In the end, the dancers were loaded onto wagons and taken to that saint's shrine in the hills.
Priests gave them red shoes, and by September, the dancing stopped.
The cure for the dancing plague wasn't a band. It was new shoes.

## Notes
- Hook = a dancing disease cured by dancing; setting = Strasbourg, one woman, no music; escalation = days → 30 → 400 →
  the city builds a stage, a band and strong men; snap = Luckily/Unluckily, it worked on everyone; reversal = the ban;
  analogy = the yawn; twist/callback = the cure was red shoes, not a band.
- No deaths claimed (disputed). "By some counts" keeps the 400 honest.
