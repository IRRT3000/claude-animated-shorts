# "The Teaspoon That Calmed a Pond": original script v1 (2026-11-03)
Format: cartoon-3d-short (~60-65 s, ~3.7 words/s), one steady voice, English only, no captions. Our own characters and story.

## Facts (checked 2026-11-03)
- 1757: Franklin, sailing in a large fleet, notices the wakes behind two ships are unusually smooth; the captain says
  the cooks have probably just emptied greasy water overboard. (Franklin's letter to William Brownrigg, 7 Nov 1773,
  published in Philosophical Transactions 64, 1774; fleet of ~96 ships bound for Louisbourg — Physics Today, "Oil on troubled waters")
- Before 1773 (exact date uncertain; letter dated 7 Nov 1773): on a windy day at a pond on Clapham Common, London, he pours "not more than a teaspoonful" of oil; it "produced an instant calm over a space several yards square, which spread amazingly" and made "all that quarter of the pond, perhaps half an acre, as smooth as a looking glass". (same letter)
- He carried oil in the hollow upper joint of his bamboo walking cane to repeat the trick. (same letter)
- Physics: the oil spreads into a film about one molecule thick and damps the small capillary ripples the wind needs
  to grow waves. ~2 cm³ over ~2,000 m² ≈ 1 nm thick. Lord Rayleigh (1890) used oil films to estimate molecular size;
  later writers did the division on Franklin's own numbers. (Rayleigh, Proc. R. Soc. 47, 1890)

## Script
Benjamin Franklin once stopped the waves on a pond with a single teaspoon of oil.
Years earlier, sailing with a fleet, he'd noticed that behind two of the ships, the sea was strangely calm.
The captain just shrugged. The cooks had probably thrown out the greasy dishwater.
Most people would forget that. Franklin carried that question around for years.
Then, on a windy day in London, he walked up to a pond full of waves, and tipped in one teaspoon of oil.
It didn't just sit there. It raced across the water, wider, and wider, and wider,
until half an acre of pond was as smooth as a mirror.
Luckily, the trick worked every time.
Unluckily for his friends, he started carrying the oil hidden inside his walking stick.
You see, wind can't push flat water. It needs tiny ripples to grab first.
Ever tried to pick up a bedsheet that's pulled perfectly tight? There's nothing to hold.
The oil spreads into a skin just one molecule thick, and it smooths every ripple before the wind can catch it.
And here's the twist: one teaspoon, half an acre. Do the division, and that skin is a millionth of a millimetre thin.
Franklin had measured the size of a molecule on a London pond.
He just never did the maths.
