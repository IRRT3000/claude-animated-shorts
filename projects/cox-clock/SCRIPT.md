# "The Clock Nobody Had to Wind": original script v1 (2026-11-04)
Format: cartoon-3d-short (~60-65 s, ~3.7 words/s), one steady voice, English only, no captions. Our own characters and story.

## Facts (checked 2026-11-04)
- Made in London in the 1760s (V&A dates it c. 1765) by jeweller/showman James Cox with his chief mechanic
  John Joseph Merlin. (V&A collections O297335; Wikipedia "Cox's timepiece")
- Cox advertised it as "The Perpetual Motion", the only working perpetual-motion machine. (V&A; Princeton Rare Books blog 2008)
- Power: a giant barometer, ~68 kg (150 lb) of mercury in two large glass jars. As air pressure rises and falls the
  mercury moves, and that motion winds the clock. No one ever has to wind it. (V&A; Wikipedia)
- Shown 1768-1774 in Cox's Mechanical Museum, Spring Gardens / Charing Cross, London; later offered as a prize in
  a museum lottery (1774-75 engraving). (V&A; Romantic Circles gallery "Mr Cox's perpetual motion, prize museum lottery")
- Bought by the V&A in 1961; deactivated when it went to the museum. (Wikipedia; V&A)
- Not real perpetual motion: the energy comes from the weather (pressure changes). (Univ. of Houston "Engines of Our Ingenuity" ep. 527)
- TO CHECK before voice: whether it is ever run today; exact museum location (Spring Gardens vs Charing Cross wording).

## Script
In the seventeen-sixties, a London jeweller built a clock that nobody ever had to wind.
His name was James Cox, and he called it, quite modestly, The Perpetual Motion.
People paid to walk into his museum and stare at it.
No key. No crank. No weights to pull up. And still, it kept on ticking.
Day after day. Week after week. Month after month.
Was it magic? A hidden servant? A very patient cat?
Cox just smiled. It was so impressive he offered it as a prize in a lottery.
Luckily, the clock never needed winding.
Unluckily, it needed weather.
You see, inside the case sat a giant barometer, with about seventy kilos of mercury.
Every time the air pressure changed, the mercury rose or fell, just a little, and that tiny push wound the clock.
Think of it like your chest when you breathe. The air pushes in, the air pulls out.
Cox's clock was breathing the sky.
So it wasn't perpetual motion. It was powered by every cloud and storm that rolled over London.
Today it sits in the Victoria and Albert Museum, the clock that nobody ever had to wind.
And here's the twist: they switched it off.
