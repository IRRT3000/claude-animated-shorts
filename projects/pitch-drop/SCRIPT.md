# "The Drop Nobody Has Ever Seen": original script v1 (2026-11-04)
Format: cartoon-3d-short (~60-65 s, ~3.7 words/s), one steady voice, English only, no captions. Our own characters and story.

## Facts (checked 2026-11-04)
- Started 1927 at the University of Queensland by its first physics professor, Thomas Parnell: heated pitch poured
  into a glass funnel with a sealed stem, left three years to settle; stem cut in 1930. Purpose: show that something
  that looks solid can be a very viscous fluid. (UQ News 2005-10-07 & 2014-11-10 explainer; Macleans 2013)
- Pitch is so brittle that "if you hit it with a hammer, it would shatter like glass" (Mainstone, quoted by Improbable Research).
- First drop 1938 (~8 years after the cut), second 1947 (9 years later); about one drop per decade. Eighth drop 28 Nov 2000.
  (UQ News; Wikipedia "Pitch drop experiment" via search)
- Viscosity estimated after the 2000 drop: ~230 billion times water. (Wikipedia via search; everything-everywhere.com)
- John Mainstone, custodian 1961-2013 (52 years), never saw a drop fall: missed one by a day (1977; some accounts say 1979,
  so no year is spoken), missed 1988 by about five minutes when he stepped out for a drink (World Expo, Brisbane),
  and in 2000 a camera was set up but a ~20-minute power outage stopped it recording the drop.
  (UQ News; Improbable Research; Gizmodo/CNN 2012; scienceblog.com)
- 2005 Ig Nobel Prize in Physics to Mainstone and the late Parnell. (UQ News 2005-10-07; improbable.com)
- Mainstone died 13 Aug 2013 after a stroke. The ninth drop touched the pile below in April 2014, still attached, and
  separated on 24 April 2014 while staff were replacing the beaker (the base wobbled when the bell jar was lifted).
  (UQ News 2014-04-17 and 2014-04-24; Improbable Research 2014-04-17)
- No one has ever watched a drop fall live. (UQ; scienceblog.com)

## Script
In nineteen twenty-seven, a professor started an experiment. Nearly a hundred years later, nobody has ever seen it happen.
His name was Thomas Parnell, and he wanted to prove that something solid could secretly be a liquid.
So he took pitch, a tar so hard that if you hit it with a hammer, it shatters like glass.
He melted it, poured it into a funnel, and waited three years for it to settle.
Then he cut the bottom open. And waited.
Eight years later, one drop fell.
Nine years after that, another. Then another. About one drop every ten years.
Luckily, the experiment got a devoted new guardian, John Mainstone, who watched over it for fifty-two years.
Unluckily, he never saw a drop fall.
Once, he missed it by a day.
Once, he stepped out for a drink, and missed it by five minutes.
Then they finally pointed a camera at it, and the drop fell during a twenty-minute power cut.
You see, pitch really is a liquid. It's just more than two hundred billion times thicker than water.
Think of cold honey on a spoon, and then give it a few decades.
The experiment even won an Ig Nobel Prize.
In twenty thirteen, John Mainstone passed away, still waiting.
And here's the twist: the next drop never really fell. It got so long it touched the pile below, and only broke off when somebody bumped the jar.

## Beat map
Hook (nobody has ever seen it) → Setting (1927, Parnell, solid is liquid) → Action (hammer shatters, melt/pour/3 yrs,
cut and wait, drop after 8 yrs, 9 yrs, every decade) → Luckily/Unluckily (guardian 52 yrs / never saw one)
→ Escalation of misses ×3 (a day, five minutes, power cut) → Explanation (230 billion × water, cold honey)
→ Consequence (Ig Nobel) → Callback twist (bumped jar).

## Visual notes
1920s/30s university physics room: wooden bench, glass bell jar over a funnel on a tripod, beaker of black drops below,
big wall clock and a calendar shedding pages for time jumps. Parnell (tweed, moustache) and Mainstone (white hair,
glasses, cardigan) as our own bean-head characters. The three misses staged clearly: empty chair + calendar,
a mug in the corridor, a dead camera with a lightning storm outside. Final: staff lifting the bell jar, a wobble, *plip*.
